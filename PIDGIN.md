# How Crisis speaks: Nigerian English and Pidgin

Reference for writing and generating dialogue. Built from research (sources at the end). Items marked *unverified* come from general usage without a source found yet: check them with a native speaker before leaning on them.

## 1. The big picture

- **English is the default on campus.** Educated Nigerians prefer English in most talk, but use Pidgin "significantly" and switch by situation, within and between sentences (Agbo 2022). It is not a class split: students, lecturers and pastors all use Pidgin informally.
- **Pidgin is the informal register**: friends, jokes, anger, the market, the hostel. English is for class, office, church, authority, strangers in service.
- **Switching to Pidgin signals closeness**; staying in English keeps distance or status.
- **Region matters.** Pidgin is deepest in the South-South (Warri, Sapele, Port Harcourt, Benin). Lagos Pidgin carries Yoruba words; eastern Pidgin carries Igbo; northern speech carries Hausa and Arabic words and is often more English-leaning. Abuja is mixed: English, Hausa and Pidgin side by side.
- **Older working people** (cooks, porters, security, market women) use Pidgin as their everyday voice. Students use English with Pidgin phrases and campus slang.

## 2. Levels (every character has one)

| Level | Who | How they talk |
|---|---|---|
| **4 · Pidgin first** | Older working people (Mama Ada, Mr. Bello) | Pidgin is their normal voice, with mother-tongue words. English only for something formal or quoted. |
| **3 · Mixed** | (not used in the party for now) | Switches freely in both directions. |
| **2 · English, Pidgin with friends** | Some students (Dan, Obinna, Bashir, Ngozi) | English sentences; a Pidgin phrase or tag when joking, angry, scared, or with peers. Never in front of elders unless upset. |
| **1 · English, Pidgin under pressure** | Most students, the lecturer | English. A Pidgin exclamation or phrase only at high emotion (fear, grief, fury) or a joke. |
| **0 · English** | Formal, pious or very correct characters (Amina, Tunde, Hauwa) | Nigerian English (sorry, well done, sir/ma) with almost no Pidgin. A single exclamation at most. |

Everyone, at every level, still sounds Nigerian: Nigerian English politeness and expressions, mother-tongue exclamations, faith phrases.

## 3. When people switch (triggers)

- **Up into Pidgin:** anger, insult, fear, a joke, teasing, solidarity with a friend, talking to an older working person in their register, food and money talk, gossip.
- **Down into English:** speaking to an elder or authority (add sir/ma), formal requests, grief at its most serious, prayer, giving instructions.
- **Inside a sentence (educated speakers):** an English sentence with a Pidgin tag ("…abi?", "…sha", "…o"); an English frame with a Pidgin predicate ("I go deal with am"); a Pidgin frame with an English noun ("E dey stress me for this assignment").
- One or two Pidgin touches per line is plenty for levels 1–2.

## 4. Grammar (for level 3–4 speech and any Pidgin line)

Spelling convention for the game: **dey, don, go, na, be, no, neva, wetin, am, im, dem, una, abeg, sabi, chop, comot, pikin, wahala**. Keep it consistent.

- **Pronouns:** I / me (written *me* or *mi* as object: "give me"), you, *e* or *im* (he/she/it as subject), *am* (him/her/it as object: "I see am"), we, *una* (you all), *dem* (they / them). No gender. Possessive: my, your, *im* (his/her), our, *una*, *dem*.
- **Three kinds of "is":**
  - *be* = what something is: "I be nurse." "Wetin be dis?"
  - *na* = it is / it was (focus): "Na Audu own." "Na emergency." Not "I na student".
  - *dey* = where, or a state: "I dey house." "I dey fine."
- **Tense and aspect** (markers go between subject and verb):
  - plain verb = past or completed by default: "I go town" (I went to town).
  - *don* = has/already: "I don chop." "E don comot."
  - *dey* = doing / usually does: "I dey come." "I no dey chop pork."
  - *go* = will (never past): "I go call police."
  - *bin* = had / used to (rare).
  - *fit* = can / may: "I fit go?" *wan* = want to / about to. *make* = let / so that: "Make we go." "Cook make I chop."
  - Order when stacked: go → don → dey → verb: "You go don chop finish."
- **Negation:** *no* before the verb or marker: "I no go." "I no sabi am." "E no fit." "No be me." *neva* = not yet / didn't (replaces *no* + *don*): "I neva chop." No double negatives: "I no see anybody", not "nobody".
- **Questions:** *wetin* (what), *who*, *which* ("which one"), *where* / *which side*, *how*, *wetin make* (why), *when* / *which time*, *how much*. Tags: *abi?* (right?), *shebi* (isn't it so…), northern *ba?* ("You no wan come, ba?").
- **Plural:** add *dem* after the noun ("di pikin dem"). *di* = the, only when it matters which one; not every noun.
- **Serial verbs:** "carry am go", "take knife cut am", "follow me go", "buy am give you", "comot go", "e big pass mine".
- **Common verbs:** chop (eat), waka (walk), comot (leave / get out), sabi (know / know how), carry (take), vex (be angry), chook (poke / stab), fit (can), yarn and gist (talk, gossip).

## 5. Particles, exclamations, address

- **Particles:** *o* (softens or emphasises: "Yes o", "Sorry o"), *sha* (anyway / regardless), *abeg* (please), *oya* (come on, let's go), *jare / joor* (dismissive or pleading), *sef* (even: "Wetin you be sef?"), *now* at the end (persuasive: "Come now"), *nko?* (what about…?: "Your mama nko?").
- **Exclamations (with origin):** *haba* (Hausa: come on / no way, protest, disbelief), *kai* (Hausa: surprise, pity, anger, pain), *chai / chei* (Igbo: surprise, dismay), *walahi* (Hausa/Arabic: truly, I swear), *ehen* (okay / and so? / go on), *omo* (Yoruba: wow / damn), *chineke* (Igbo: God!), *ah-ah* (surprise or protest, *unverified spelling*), *wahala* (trouble).
- **Address:** *oga* (boss, sir), *madam*, *mama* / *papa* (to elders), *aunty* / *uncle* (any older person), *my guy*, *bros*, *my sister*, *sir / ma* (respect, attached to sentences: "Yes ma").
- **Nigerian English that is not Pidgin:** "sorry" (sympathy, not apology), "well done" (to someone working or suffering, *unverified*), "I'm coming" (I'll be right back, even when leaving), "how far" (hello / what's up), "no vex" (don't be upset / sorry), "goodnight ma" (leaving for the day), "next tomorrow" (the day after tomorrow).

## 6. Who would use what

- **Older people** use: wahala, oga, abeg, oya, ehen, na so, comot, gbege, haba, chai, walahi, "my pikin", "God dey".
- **Students** use: omo, how far, e choke, japa, sapa, "on God", "e be things", campus words (jacking = reading hard, spagewa = beans and spaghetti, origo = exam cheating). Gen-Z English (L, W, salty, opp, periodt) only from the youngest, rarely.
- **Northern characters** (Bashir, Amina, Hauwa): haba, kai, walahi, toh (okay), sannu (sorry / hello), Insha Allah; English often careful. Not jokes about "Allah".
- **Ilorin (Mr. Bello):** Yoruba and Muslim: "Alhamdulillah", Yoruba exclamations, Pidgin.
- **Igbo (Mama Ada, Chidera, Emeka, Obinna):** chai, chineke, nne/nna, biko, daalu.
- **Yoruba (Tunde, Segun, Funke, Ngozi from Lagos):** e pele / pele, oya, abi, omo, jare.

## 6b. Bandits

- **Who they are:** armed rural gangs from the north-west (Kaduna side). Their own languages are Hausa and often Fulfulde; Hausa is the regional common language. With southern students they use rough Pidgin with Hausa words and short Hausa commands. Among themselves they talk in Hausa or Fulfulde, which the students do not follow.
- **Mai Kudi**, the collector, is the exception: a former Bursary clerk, calm, polite, in English. His politeness is the threat.
- **How they sound:** short, barked lines. Pidgin grammar (section 4), commands, few words. One Hausa word per line, never a long Hausa sentence (Hausa is tonal and has hooked letters; long written Hausa is easy to get wrong).
- **Verified Hausa they can use:**
  - *zo nan* = come here · *zauna* = sit down · *bari* = leave it · *kada ka (zo)* = don't (come), *ka* to a man, *ki* to a woman
  - *kudi* = money ("Kana da kudi?" = you have money?)
  - *haba* = come on / no way (protest, disbelief) · *kai* = surprise, anger, pain · *walahi* = I swear, truly
  - *shikenan* (southern ear: *shikena*) = that's it, it's done · *sannu* = hello (from a bandit, mocking)
  - *shege* = bastard (strong insult; also an intensifier) · *mugu* = fool (in Pidgin) · *wahala* = trouble
  - *sai gobe* = until tomorrow (southern ears hear "trouble is coming")
- **Unverified, keep out until a native speaker checks:** kar, wawa, banza, maza, yanzu, bindiga, toh, yauwa, and any long Hausa command.
- **Do not** put "Allah" in a threat or a joke; do not use *uwar ka / waaka* (your mother) unless a scene truly needs that weight.
- **The kid** (bandit_kid) is young and frightened under the gun: more Hausa when scared (he cries for his mother in Hausa), short broken Pidgin.

## 7. What makes it sound fake (avoid)

- *dey* as every "is", *di* before every noun.
- *na* in the wrong place ("I na student").
- *go* used for the past ("I go there yesterday" means will, not went).
- Double negatives.
- Word-for-word swaps of English ("How you are?" instead of "How you dey?").
- Too many particles in one line (abeg… o… sha… jare). One per line.
- Slang dumping: a list of slang in one breath reads like a glossary.
- Making every poor character speak Pidgin and every educated one speak English.
- Over-polished, formal English for young characters: let them be quick and blunt.
